What this teaches, and what it doesn't

This is a guide to playing the song — the harmony, the voicings, the left hand, and a week of practice that gets you from cold to playing it through. It is not a transcription. The tune is still in copyright, so the melody and lyrics aren't printed here; buy the sheet music if you want them on the page, or do what jazz and cabaret players actually do and put the melody on top by ear, which the last section walks you through.

That order is not a compromise. Every good player of this repertoire learns the changes first and the tune second, because the changes are what let you accompany a singer, play it in another key, stretch the time, or stop mid-phrase without falling apart.

On the harmony below. Published editions of this song differ — the 1948 show score, vocal editions, and jazz fake books all voice it slightly differently. What follows is a playable reduction: it keeps the two harmonic devices the song is actually built on, and simplifies the rest so it fits two octaves and a modest hand. If you buy a lead sheet, expect small differences, and trust the printed one.

Everything fits here

The whole arrangement lives between C3 and E5 — a bit under two and a half octaves. Left hand stays in the octave below middle C; right hand sits from middle C upward. If your keyboard is 49 keys or more, you have room. If it's smaller, transpose the whole thing up or down an octave; nothing here depends on absolute pitch.

Throughout, gold keys are the left hand and rose keys are the right. Press Play on any diagram to hear it.

Lesson 1

The falling line

Hold an F minor chord. Now move exactly one note inside it downward, one key at a time: F → E → E♭ → D. The chord stays F minor the whole way; only that single voice slides. Four chords, four bars, one moving finger.

Musicians call this a line cliché, which undersells it badly. It is the sound of something beautiful quietly coming apart, and Porter opens the song with it, returns to it, and closes on it. If you only ever learn one thing from this page, learn these four bars — they are more than half of the song's total material.

Left hand holds low F. Right hand: thumb on F, fingers 2 and 5 on A♭ and C. Only the thumb moves.

Fingering

Right hand: thumb (1) on the moving note, finger 2 on A♭, finger 5 on C. Set that shape once and don't reset it — the thumb walks down four white-and-black keys while 2 and 5 stay planted. Left hand plays a single low F and holds it, or re-strikes it softly on each bar.

Small hands: if F–up-to-C is a reach, drop the top C and play just two notes in the right hand. The line still reads perfectly.

Lesson 2

Your ten chords

The entire song runs on these. Each one is a single left-hand root plus three right-hand notes — no chord here needs more than a sixth in the right hand. The right-hand notes deliberately skip the root, which is why they sound like a record and not like a beginner's book.

Learn them in the three groups below, in order. Don't try to memorise all ten in a sitting.

Group 1 · Home (F minor and its falling line)

Group 2 · The turn toward the light

These four take you out of F minor toward A♭ major — its relative major, the "warm" key hiding inside the sad one — and then C7 drags you back home. This is the second half of every A section.

Group 3 · The bridge and the ending

The bridge lifts to D♭ major, the flattened sixth — the classic "wide open sky" chord in a minor key. The ending is where Porter twists the knife: the last chord is F major. Same root, one note different, entirely different feeling.

The map

Thirty-two bars

Four strains of eight bars. The first, second, and fourth are the same song; the third is the bridge. Notice how little there is to memorise once you see the repeats — bars 1–4, 9–12 and 25–28 are all the falling line, and bars 5–8, 13–16 and 21–24 are the same four chords.

Click any bar to hear it.

Bar 30 is the only bar with three chords in it — B♭m, B♭m7, B♭m6, roughly a beat and a half each. It's the falling line again, transplanted onto the minor fourth chord, right before the final cadence. Porter is quoting himself as the song ends.

Lesson 3

What the left hand does

Three levels. Play level one until the chart is automatic, then upgrade. Rushing to level three before the changes are memorised is the single most common way people stall on this tune.

Level 1 — one note, held

Play the root on beat 1 and hold it for the whole bar. That's it. It sounds better than you expect, because the right hand is carrying real harmony. Use it for your first three or four days.

Level 2 — root and fifth

Root on beat 1, the fifth below it on beat 3. For F minor that's F3 then C3. It gives the ballad a slow pulse without adding notes to think about. Keep the fifth quieter than the root.

Level 3 — a spread ballad figure

Roll root → fifth → root-an-octave-up across the first two beats, then let it ring. On F minor: F3, C4, F4 — but move the right hand up an octave first so you aren't fighting for the same keys. This is the sound of the classic vocal accompaniment, and it wants a soft, unhurried touch, closer to a harp than a stride.

Pedal

One pedal change per chord, on the beat the chord arrives — press just after you strike, not with it, or the change smears. In bar 30, where three chords go by, change three times. If your keyboard has no sustain pedal, hold the left-hand note with your finger instead and let it sing.

Lesson 4

Putting the tune on top

Once the chart is in your hands, add the melody — and the honest way to learn it is with your ears, not with a page.

  1. Listen until you can sing it. Properly sing it, unaccompanied, all thirty-two bars. If you can't, you're not ready to find it on the keys; go back to the recordings.
  2. Find the first note. Play your F minor chord, then hunt for the melody's opening pitch. It's inside that chord. Everything else follows from getting that one right.
  3. Work one strain at a time. Play the chord for the bar, sing the phrase, then find it. The melody sits mostly between C4 and F5, and it moves in small steps far more than it leaps.
  4. Then rebalance your hands. Melody in the right hand's top finger, played louder than everything else. Move the three-note voicings down into the left hand, or split them between the two.

This is slow the first time and much faster the second. It is also the skill that makes every future standard easier, which a printed page never gives you.

Seven days

A practice plan

Twenty to thirty minutes a day. The order matters — each day depends on the one before it, so don't skip ahead even if a day feels easy.

Day 1
The falling line only

Bars 1–4, hands together, left hand holding one note. Slowly, until the thumb walks down without you watching it. Nothing else today.

Day 2
Add the turn

Learn Group 2 — B♭m7, E♭7, A♭maj7, C7 — as isolated shapes. Then play the full first eight bars, badly, at whatever speed lets you not stop.

Day 3
Sixteen bars

A and A′ back to back. They're nearly identical, so this is mostly a memory day. Metronome at 60; the goal is not stopping, not sounding good.

Day 4
The bridge

Bars 17–24. D♭maj7 and G♭maj7 are new shapes; give them ten minutes alone before joining them up. Then bars 1–24 straight through.

Day 5
The ending

Bars 25–32, especially bar 30's three chords and the final F major. Play the last four bars twenty times. It's the moment people remember.

Day 6
All thirty-two, and the pedal

Whole form, slowly, with pedal changes. Upgrade the left hand to level 2. Expect it to fall apart at the bridge — that's normal on day 6.

Day 7
Make it music

Take the metronome off. Play it at the speed the song wants, which is slower than you think. Get quieter in the bridge, and let the last chord ring until it's gone. Then start finding the melody.
